Bike Sharing Case Study: "Should we just buy more bikes?"¶

The CEO's problem: riders can't find a bike when they need one, yet bikes sit unused around the city. Do we buy more?

My first thought: if there's a shortage and idle bikes at the same time, the problem is probably when and where the bikes are, not how many there are. This notebook checks that with the data.

2. Feature understanding¶

  • Time (hr, weekday, workingday, holiday, mnth, season): the main drivers, because people commute at fixed hours.
  • Trend (yr): the system grew a lot from 2011 to 2012.
  • Weather (weathersit, temp, hum, windspeed): converted back to °C, %, and km/h so they're readable.
  • User type (casual vs registered): can't be used for prediction, but helps explain who rides when.

Engineered feature: hr_day = hour × day type (e.g. 8_1 = 8 AM on a working day). Section 3 shows why it matters.

3. Exploration¶

This chart explains most of the CEO's problem:

  • Working days: two sharp peaks (8 AM and 5–6 PM), almost all registered users, i.e. commuters.
  • Weekends: one smooth midday hump with many more casual riders.

In the morning commuters ride from homes to downtown, so residential stations empty out and downtown docks fill up. In the evening it reverses. That's "no bike where I am" and "bikes sitting unused over there" at the same time, with the same fleet.

It also shows that the hour effect depends on the day type. 8 AM is the busiest hour on a working day but a quiet one on a weekend. A model that adds "hour" and "working day" separately can't capture that, which is why I built the hr_day interaction.

  • Growth: 2012 is ~65% busier than 2011. This is the only real argument for buying bikes.
  • Season and temperature: summer/fall are 2–3x winter, and demand rises with temperature up to ~30 °C.

4. Hypothesis¶

H1: The shortage is a timing problem, not a fleet-size problem. On working days, rentals in peak hours (7–9 AM, 4–7 PM) are significantly higher than in off-peak daytime hours (10 AM–3 PM).

  • H0: peak-hour rentals are not higher than off-peak.
  • Test: Mann–Whitney U, one-sided. Hourly counts are skewed, so I don't assume normality. I also report the effect size, since with thousands of rows almost anything is "significant".
  • Control check: if the peaks really come from commuting, the pattern should disappear on weekends.
Working days     peak median  353 | off-peak median  175 | ratio 2.02x | p-value 0.0e+00
Weekends/holiday peak median  173 | off-peak median  336 | ratio 0.51x | p-value 1.0e+00

Result: on working days, the median peak hour has ~2x the rentals of the median off-peak hour (p ≈ 0), so H0 is rejected. On weekends the ratio flips to 0.5x, which confirms the spike comes from commuters.

What it means: a fleet sized for the 8 AM / 5 PM spike will have lots of idle bikes the rest of the day.

How I'd test it in the real world¶

The data has city-wide counts only, with no station-level information, so the business decision should come from a pilot:

  • Treatment: fill residential stations before 7 AM, clear full downtown docks mid-morning, and make sure downtown has enough bikes before the 5 PM rush.
  • Control: business as usual.
  • Randomisation: ~20 neighbourhood clusters of stations, half assigned to each group. Clusters rather than single stations, because riders walk to nearby stations.
  • Duration: 4–6 weeks of working days.
  • Primary metric: minutes stations sit empty (or full) during peaks.
  • Secondary: rides per bike, complaints. Guardrail: rebalancing cost per extra ride.
  • Decision: if empty-station minutes drop meaningfully (e.g. ≥20%) at a lower cost than buying bikes, rebalance first.

5. Demand model¶

  • Target: rentals per hour, modelled as log(rentals + 1) because counts are skewed and errors grow with volume.
  • Split by time: train Jan 2011–Aug 2012, test Sep–Dec 2012. A random split would let the model peek at neighbouring hours and look better than it is.

Models, from simplest to most complex:

  1. Baseline: historical average for the same hour, day type and year
  2. Linear regression: one-hot hour, month, season, weekday and weather, plus temperature, humidity, wind, year and holiday
  3. Linear regression + hour × day-type interaction: same as 2, but hour is replaced by hr_day
  4. Random forest: same raw features, no one-hot needed

The interaction is the most important modelling choice in this notebook.

  • Plain linear regression is worse than the simple baseline. It has to use one hour shape for every day, so it gets both the commute peaks and the weekend hump wrong.
  • Adding the hour × day-type interaction cuts the error almost in half, and beats the random forest on every metric.

So I pick linear regression with the interaction as the final model. It's the most accurate here, and it's also the easiest to explain: every coefficient is "how much this hour, month or weather changes demand". It's fast and doesn't need tuning.

The coefficients reproduce the two shapes from the exploration (commute peaks vs the weekend hump), so the model is learning the real pattern.

A planning number, not just an average¶

If ops plans for the expected demand, they'll run short about half the time. So I add a simple buffer: in 90% of training hours, actual demand was below prediction × buffer.

90th percentile of actual/predicted on training data: 1.50  (used as 1.5 in model_utils.py)
On the unseen test months, actual demand was below the planning number in 95% of hours

On the test months the buffer covered ~95% of hours, a bit more than the 90% target. So it's slightly conservative, which is the safer direction when the cost of a shortage is an unhappy rider.

Limitations:

  • The model uses actual weather. In real use it would use the weather forecast, which adds some error.
  • yr is just a 2011 vs 2012 switch, so it can't predict growth into 2013. That needs a separate growth adjustment.
  • Only one interaction. Others (e.g. hour × weather, since rain hurts leisure rides more than commutes) could help, but I kept the model simple.

For the fleet numbers and the assistant, I retrain on all the data.

6. How many bikes are needed?¶

The data counts rentals, not bikes, so I convert with Little's Law:

bikes on the road at once = rentals per hour × average trip length (hours)

bikes needed = bikes on the road ÷ 70% max utilisation (so some bikes stay docked and findable)

20-minute trips and 70% utilisation are assumptions, so I also show how the result changes if they're wrong.

This is the core answer for the CEO.

  • Even at the busiest hours, only a few hundred bikes are needed system-wide (with my assumptions).
  • A fleet sized for those hours is more than half idle in most hours, and only about a fifth of it is in use on average.
  • So riders aren't short of bikes in the city. They're short of bikes at their station at 8 AM. More bikes would mostly add to the idle pile in the wrong places.

The company's real fleet size isn't in the data. Once ops shares it, actual utilisation falls straight out of this.

7. Recommendation¶

Short answer to the CEO: not yet. Fix where the bikes are before buying more.

  1. Rebalance around the commute on working days: fill residential stations before 7 AM, clear full downtown docks mid-morning so arriving commuters can park, and keep enough bikes downtown for the 5 PM rush home. On weekends, stock parks and leisure spots by mid-morning.
  2. Plan with the forecast: each evening, run the model on tomorrow's weather to plan trucks and staff.
  3. Run the pilot (section 4) before spending on bikes.
  4. Buy for growth, not peaks: demand grew ~65% in a year, so the fleet will need to grow eventually, sized from the planning forecast and measured utilisation.
  5. Collect the missing data: station dock status every few minutes, failed unlock attempts and trip durations.

Assumptions¶

  • 20-minute average trip and 70% max utilisation (see the sensitivity table).
  • Missing night hours ≈ zero demand. The Sandy days were real shutdowns.
  • 2012 patterns represent the near future, apart from growth.
